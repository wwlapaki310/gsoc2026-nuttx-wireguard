Let's start with part one: an overview and the background.

Project Overview

Packets sent to wg0 are encrypted and sent out as UDP. UDP that comes in is decrypted and handed over as if it came in on wg0. The other side can be any normal WireGuard peer.

Takeaway

Apps see wg0 as a normal network interface. Only the "wire" under it is different: a UDP socket, not a cable.

PART 1 · 1/65 / 40
Here is the whole idea in one picture. || Apache NuttX is an RTOS (real-time operating system) that follows POSIX (the Portable Operating System Interface) and has its own TCP/IP stack. It had no VPN. This project adds wg0 as a virtual network interface. Applications see a normal interface. Under it, the encrypted data travels over a UDP socket. The other side is a real WireGuard peer — Linux, Windows, or another gateway. || Because NuttX follows POSIX and uses BSD sockets, it is a good fit for this work. FreeRTOS and Zephyr can run a VPN too. But on NuttX, a VPN can become a normal network device very naturally.

Problem: Wi-Fi security stops too early

Open it to the Internet

Give the device a public IP and hope.

Make your own protocol

Build and maintain your own secure channel.

Use a vendor's cloud

Rely on someone else's cloud to reach your own device.

Takeaway

What was missing: a standard, secure path all the way to the NuttX device.

PART 2 · 1/412 / 40
Now, the problem itself. || Wi-Fi security is real, but it protects only one part of the path: the radio link between the device and the access point. Everything before that — the engineer, the Internet, the router — is outside what WPA2 or WPA3 (Wi-Fi security) can protect. What I needed was a secure path for the whole way, from the engineer to the device, not just the last few meters. || Without that, every choice has a problem. You can give the device a public IP and hope. You can make your own secure protocol and keep it working forever. Or you can hand the connection to a vendor's cloud. The same problem appears in edge AI, industrial IoT, and remote systems in general. It is not limited to this project.

PART 2 · 2/413 / 40
Before I chose WireGuard, I compared the usual ways to reach a device. || Opening a port is easy, but then the whole Internet can reach the device. HTTPS and MQTT (a small messaging protocol) are what most IoT products use. The device connects out to a server, so NAT (network address translation) is not a problem, and NuttX already has clients for both. But you only get the messages you designed — you cannot log in or open a web page on the device. And the security ends at the broker or the cloud. || A reverse tunnel gives you one port through a relay server. A VPN on the router is great when the router is yours. But the last part of the path, on the LAN, is not protected. And at a customer's site, the router is usually not yours. A carrier closed network works only over cellular, and you need a contract. || A VPN on the device is the only row that has all three: any IP service, no server in the middle, and security all the way to the device. MQTT and HTTPS are still the right tools for data. This is for maintenance access.

Solution: a VPN on the device

  • Modern IP-layer VPN small and simple: much less code than IPsec or OpenVPN.
  • UDP transport, public-key peers no certificates and no session setup to manage.
  • Already everywhere in the Linux kernel, with an official Windows client. It works with existing peers, not a custom protocol.
Takeaway

WireGuard solves the access problem with a standard protocol, not a homemade security layer or a vendor cloud.

PART 2 · 3/414 / 40
So, why WireGuard? || WireGuard is a modern VPN that works at the IP layer. It is small and simple by design, with much less code than IPsec or OpenVPN, and that matters a lot on a microcontroller. It runs over plain UDP, and each peer is known by its public key, not by certificates or a long setup. If a packet does not carry the right key, WireGuard does not answer at all — to everyone else, the device is just a brick wall. And most importantly, it is a standard. It has been in the Linux kernel for years, there is an official Windows client, and the other end can be any WireGuard peer. || So I chose a standard protocol, instead of making my own or depending on a vendor's cloud.

Architecture: where wg0 sits

Takeaway

To NuttX, wg0 is a normal network device. Under it, WireGuard turns its packets into encrypted UDP.

PART 2 · 4/415 / 40
This is one step more technical than the overview. This is where the implementation starts. || Going out, a packet comes down from the application through TCP or UDP and IP, and reaches wg0 like any other network device. WireGuard encrypts it, and it leaves as a UDP packet — through wlan0 on a board, or through usrsock when the Wi-Fi chip itself works through sockets. Coming in is the same path the other way: a UDP packet arrives, WireGuard decrypts it, and it is passed up to wg0 as if it came in on a normal interface. || From here on, the work is building this middle part — wg0 and the encryption — without changing the layers above or below it.

Part three: the plan. How do you reuse an existing WireGuard?

Porting: four functions to write

Protocol & crypto — KEEP

Handshake, transport, BLAKE2s, ChaCha20-Poly1305, Curve25519. Portable C, no OS dependency. Crypto used as-is; the protocol core got two small, documented fixes.

Platform hooks — ADAPT

Everything OS-specific sits behind wireguard-platform.h — four functions: clock, random bytes, TAI64N time, load check.

lwIP network glue — REPLACE

wireguardif.c's lwIP netif registration and buffers. NuttX has no lwIP, so this layer is the real porting work.

Takeaway

A clean line between the protocol and the OS let the crypto move unchanged, and the protocol core with only two small fixes. Only the OS and network glue had to change.

PART 3 · 1/217 / 40
Why I started from wireguard-lwip, an existing open-source WireGuard, and did not write the crypto myself. || wireguard-lwip is cleanly split into three layers. The protocol and crypto — the handshake, the data transport, BLAKE2s, ChaCha20-Poly1305 and Curve25519 — are portable C that does not depend on any OS. I used the crypto without changes. The protocol core needed two small fixes later, after a review, and they are written down in PATCHES.md. Everything that depends on the OS was already kept behind one small header file, as four functions. I only had to replace the lwIP network glue — the part that registers an lwIP netif and handles lwIP buffers — because NuttX does not have lwIP at all. || I did not have to rewrite the protocol or the crypto, and that was not luck. That is what a clean line between the protocol and the OS gives you. Because of it, this port took weeks, not months.

Takeaway

Porting meant using NuttX's own building blocks, not bringing lwIP into NuttX.

PART 3 · 2/218 / 40
This was the most important porting decision. || Instead of bringing lwIP into NuttX, I matched each lwIP idea to NuttX's own. lwIP's network interface becomes a NuttX network driver, lwIP's packet buffers become NuttX's I/O buffers, adding an interface becomes registering a NuttX network device, and the send callback becomes NuttX's own poll. wg0 is registered as a TUN (tunnel) type network device, and the “wire” under it is a UDP socket.

PART 4 · 2/521 / 40
Now, where it was hard. || The first tunnel worked quickly. Most of my time went into six bugs: four in the apps version, and two more after I moved it into the kernel. Most of them hid behind a test that passed, like a handshake or a ping, so I first looked in the wrong place. || Bug one: the receive thread was created as detached, and it stopped when the wg command that started it exited. Detach only decides when the memory is freed, not how long the thread lives. That is why, in the kernel version, the driver owns its thread. Bug four: only real hardware showed it. On the board, the receive task's stack was ninety-four percent full, with only 176 bytes left. Sim has big stacks, so it never saw it. Please remember this one. || I will tell two of them in detail, because their lessons are useful for anyone. Bugs five and six come in part five. The logs and fixes for all six are in the GitHub repository.

Bug 2/6: ping works. TCP dies.

Same tunnel: ping is 0% loss. A telnet 3-way handshake even completes, then the connection dies.

wg_encrypt_and_send: psock_sendto
ret=-1 errno=9  EBADF

The sends that worked ran inside the wg_rx task. Sends from another task got EBADF.

Cause: an fd belongs to one task group; it is not global.

Fix: use the internal API without fds: psock_*() on a struct socket, which any task can use.

Takeaway

If only some tasks fail, look at what each task owns. Here, it was the file descriptor.

PART 4 · 3/522 / 40
This is my favorite bug in the project. || On the same tunnel, ping was perfect, and even a TCP handshake finished — and then the connection died. When I added logging to the send path, it showed EBADF: bad file descriptor. All the sends that worked happened inside the receive task itself — handshake replies, keepalives, ping replies, the SYN-ACK (the second step of a TCP connection). Sends from any other task failed. || The reason: in NuttX, a file descriptor belongs to one task group. It is not shared by the whole system. The fix was to stop using file descriptors and use NuttX's internal socket API. It works on a struct socket, which is just memory, so any task can use it. That is exactly why the kernel version keeps the socket as a struct socket.

Bug 3/6: “No real impact” was 10×.

Quick fix: check the socket every 50 ms.

The fix without fds also broke poll(). I thought the only cost was waking an idle CPU. Wrong: each packet waited up to 50 ms, and that made it about 10× slower.

Real fix: a callback on psock_poll() and a semaphore, a real blocking wait.

Takeaway

A quick fix you never measure can look free. Measure it.

PART 4 · 4/523 / 40
The third: something I never measured. || The fix without file descriptors also broke poll, because poll works with file descriptors. So, as a quick fix, I checked the socket every fifty milliseconds. I thought the only cost was waking up the CPU when it was idle. I was wrong. Every packet that arrived waited up to fifty milliseconds, and that alone limited the speed — about ten times slower than it should have been. I thought it had no real cost, and it cost ten times. The real fix was a proper wait: the socket wakes the thread up when data arrives.

Why the kernel: from app to driver

Version 1: an app

Lives in apps/ and reaches into kernel internals (psock_*).

Works only in a FLAT build. It can never be a proper in-tree driver.

Version 2: a kernel driver

wg0 lives in drivers/net/wireguard/. A small wg command sets it up through ioctl.

Made for FLAT, PROTECTED and KERNEL builds.

Build modes. FLAT: apps and kernel share one memory space. PROTECTED: the MPU keeps apps out of kernel memory. KERNEL: each program has its own address space (MMU).

Takeaway

The goal: one device that fits how NuttX is built, in every build mode, in a shape the community can review.

PART 5 · 1/626 / 40
Why did I move it into the kernel? || Version one is an app. It lives in apps and reaches into socket internals of the kernel. That works in a FLAT build, where everything shares one memory space. But it can never be a proper in-tree driver, and it cannot work in PROTECTED or KERNEL builds, where apps and the kernel are kept apart. || So I moved the device into the kernel, with the other network drivers, and a small wg command sets it up through ioctl (input/output control). Version one showed that the protocol works on NuttX. Version two is about fitting NuttX itself: a normal device, in every build mode, in a shape the community can review.

The kernel design: the big picture

Takeaway

The driver owns everything that must keep running. The command only sends settings, and then it can exit.

PART 5 · 2/627 / 40
This is the shape of the real driver. || In user space, there is only a small wg command. It sends settings to the kernel through ioctl. Each ioctl carries one flat struct with no pointers inside, so in PROTECTED and KERNEL builds the kernel never has to follow a pointer into user memory. The private key is write-only: no ioctl ever gives it back. || In the kernel, wg0 is a normal network device. It owns its UDP socket and its receive thread, so the thread and socket bugs from version one cannot happen, by design. The crypto is NuttX's own. The protocol core is still derived from wireguard-lwip under BSD-3-Clause, and the LICENSE file says so.

Bugs 5 and 6: found in the kernel part

In NuttX's own crypto

chacha20poly1305 put the counter in the wrong nonce bytes. Counter 0 still matches, so the handshake works, and data packets after the first fail.

Nothing in NuttX had used it before. Two-line fix, kept as its own crypto: change.

Only in a KERNEL build

Setting the peers put a 6 KB array on a 3 KB kernel stack: overflow, then panic.

Sim (FLAT, big stacks) never hit it. Moved to the heap.

Also found in NuttX: SPRESENSE (cxd56) did not boot on NuttX 13.0.1 and master with CONFIG_RTC_HIRES. Fixed locally; the report is drafted, not sent.

Takeaway

Porting into NuttX also found bugs in NuttX. A port can give something back to the project.

PART 5 · 3/628 / 40

When the counter is zero, both layouts are the same, so the handshake works, and then data packets after the first one fail to decrypt. Nothing in NuttX had called this code before, so nobody had hit it. It is a two-line fix, kept as its own crypto change. || Second, a crash only in a kernel build. Testing: what ping misses

Same key on every boot?

SPRESENSE 3 resets, 3 different keys.

A quick check, not a proof of good randomness.

Keys left in memory after down?

sim session keys all zero after down.

The static key stays, on purpose, so up works again.

A leak that shows after hours?

sim 151 rekeys, 100 down/up: nothing lost.

Memory back to the start-up count. Not a week-long run.

Takeaway

Each risk needs its own test. A passing tunnel is not one of them.

PART 5 · 5/630 / 40
This slide is about what a working tunnel cannot tell you: three problems that ping would never notice. || First, keys. NuttX can seed its random device from build-time settings. Then a board makes the same private key every time it starts, and everything still works. So I reset a SPRESENSE three times and got three different keys. That is a quick check, not a proof of good randomness. || Second, I read the device's memory in the simulator. After down, the session keys were all zero. The static key stays on purpose, so that up works again. || Third, a long test, also in the simulator: a hundred and fifty-one forced rekeys and a hundred down-and-up cycles. No buffer was lost, and memory went back to exactly the start-up number. I am not saying it ran for a week, or that the simulator acts like the board.

Design choice: trust the clock?

Each handshake needs a newer timestamp than the peer has seen, even after a reboot.

Chosen

Real-time clock + the highest value used this boot. Same rule as Linux and wireguard-go, plus a warning when the clock was never set.

Not chosen

Save every timestamp: wears out flash.
Reserve a range in flash: needs a write guarantee I could not verify.
Wait: as long as it last ran.

Same board, nothing sent to it: clock unset → no handshake in 75 s. Clock set → 4.1 s.

Takeaway

Without a clock, a device may not reconnect by itself, and a ping test will not show it.

PART 5 · 6/631 / 40
This is the one place where I had to decide, not test. And then I measured my decision. || WireGuard's replay protection has a rule: every handshake needs a newer timestamp than the peer has seen, even after a reboot. The device is usually the side that starts the handshake, and it is also the side least likely to have a clock with a battery. || Saving every timestamp wears out the flash. Reserving a range in flash is the right long-term answer, but it needs a write guarantee I could not verify on these file systems. And just waiting means a device that ran for a month cannot reconnect for a month. Here, the easy choice and the right choice were not the same. || So I chose the real-time clock plus the highest value used in this boot, exactly what Linux and wireguard-go do. And the driver warns once if the clock was never set. || Then I measured it on the board. I sent the board nothing at all, because a ping from the peer would start the handshake for it. With the clock as it boots: no handshake in a seventy-five-second watch. With the clock set: four point one seconds. That is why the driver prints a warning.

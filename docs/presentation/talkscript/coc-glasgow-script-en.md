## 11 — Problem: How Can We Securely Access Remote Devices? (7:50 → 8:43)

**On slide:** Engineer → Internet → router → Wi-Fi → NuttX device, with a bracket for the secure path needed all the way, and WPA2/WPA3 covering only the Wi-Fi hop; three cards: devices are far away / remote diagnosis / a secure path.

**Say:**
- Now, the problem I wanted to solve.
- Embedded devices are often installed far away: at a customer's site, in a factory, or on another network. When something goes wrong, an engineer wants to log in, check the device, and fix it, without traveling there.
- Wi-Fi security, WPA2 or WPA3, protects the radio link between the device and the access point. That is important, but it is only one hop. It does not give an engineer a way to reach the device across the Internet. For that, we need a secure path of its own, all the way from the engineer to the device. The same need appears in edge AI, industrial IoT, and many other remote systems.
- So the question is: how can we securely access remote devices? Let's look at the options.

## 12 — Options: Ways to Reach a Remote Device (8:43 → 9:35)

**On slide:** A table of six ways (open a port / HTTPS or MQTT via a cloud / reverse tunnel or relay / VPN on the router / carrier closed network / VPN on the device) against: any IP service, no server in the middle, protected up to where.

**Say:**
- There are several ways to access a remote device: open a port, use a cloud API or an MQTT broker, set up a reverse tunnel, or use a VPN on the router.
- Each approach has its own trade-offs. HTTPS and MQTT are great for sending data, but they do not give you a shell or a web page on the device. A router VPN works well when the router is yours, but the protection ends at the router. None of them is bad, and all of them can be made secure. They just protect different parts of the path.
- For this project, I wanted something different: a standard VPN that runs directly on the NuttX device, so the device itself is the end of the tunnel. That is the solution.

**If asked about Tailscale or ZeroTier:** they are also a VPN on the device, but they usually need a bigger OS than a microcontroller has today (footnote on the slide).

## 13 — Solution: A VPN on the Device (9:35 → 10:35)

**On slide:** Engineer / Linux / Windows ⟷ WireGuard tunnel ⟷ NuttX device. "Why WireGuard?": small & simple (a small bar chart of lines of code: IPsec ~420k, OpenVPN ~120k, WireGuard ~3.8k, from 2018) / modern cryptography (ChaCha20-Poly1305, Curve25519) / UDP-based tunnel.

**Say:**
- So, my solution: put a VPN on the device itself.
- Why WireGuard? First, it is small and simple. In a talk in 2018, its author compared lines of code: about four thousand for WireGuard, about a hundred and twenty thousand for OpenVPN, and over four hundred thousand for IPsec with strongSwan. These are old numbers for specific implementations, but the gap is large, and it matters on a microcontroller.
- Second, modern cryptography: ChaCha20-Poly1305 and Curve25519. I will not go into the details here. Third, it is a tunnel over plain UDP. Each peer is known by its public key, and the packets are normal UDP, so the device can talk to existing WireGuard peers, such as Linux or Windows. The microcontroller itself becomes the end of the tunnel.
- So the idea is simple: run a lightweight, standard VPN directly on the NuttX device. Next, where wg0 sits inside NuttX.

**Check before the talk:** the line counts come from the WireGuard author's Linux Plumbers 2018 talk. They are old, for specific implementations, and the talk's slides give slightly different OpenVPN numbers. Say so if asked.

## 14 — Architecture: WireGuard on the NuttX Network Stack (10:35 → 11:23)

**On slide:** Application → TCP/UDP/IP → wg0 (NET_LL_TUN netdev) → WireGuard encrypt/decrypt → UDP socket → wlan0 / usrsock → network, with TX and RX arrows.

**Say:**
- This is one step more technical than the overview. This is where the implementation starts.
- Going out, a packet comes down from the application through TCP or UDP and IP, and reaches wg0 like any other network device. WireGuard encrypts it, and it leaves as a UDP packet — through wlan0 on a board, or through usrsock when the Wi-Fi chip itself works through sockets. Coming in is the same path the other way: a UDP packet arrives, WireGuard decrypts it, and it is passed up to wg0 as if it came in on a normal interface.
- From here on, the work is building this middle part — wg0 and the encryption — without changing the layers above or below it.

## 16 — Porting Strategy: Start from wireguard-lwip (11:33 → 12:38)

**On slide:** The link github.com/smartalock/wireguard-lwip (clickable), then three rows: KEEP protocol & crypto (with two small fixes) / ADAPT platform hooks / REPLACE lwIP network glue.

**Say:**
- Now the plan. I did not write WireGuard from scratch.
- I started from wireguard-lwip, an open-source WireGuard implementation for the lwIP network stack. The link is on the slide. It already has the protocol and the cryptography, so I could focus on the porting work.
- I sorted the code into three parts. First, the protocol and crypto: the handshake, the data transport, and the crypto primitives. I kept them. The crypto is used as it is, and the protocol core needed two small fixes, which I found later in a review. Second, the platform hooks. Everything that depends on the OS sits behind one small header, as four functions: a monotonic clock, random bytes, the current time in TAI64N format, and a check for whether the system is under load. I adapted these to NuttX. Third, the lwIP glue. NuttX does not have lwIP, so I replaced this layer. That is the real porting work.
- Next: how the lwIP ideas map to NuttX.

**Check before the talk:** the four platform hooks are a monotonic clock, random bytes, TAI64N time and an under-load check (`wireguard-platform.h`). Say them from the notes, not the slide.

## 17 — Mapping lwIP Networking to NuttX (12:38 → 13:24)

**On slide:** Table: network device (netif → net_driver_s) / packet buffers (pbuf → iob) / registration (netif_add() → netdev_register()) / sending (output callback → d_txavail → devif_poll()) / UDP wire (udp_pcb → UDP socket). A note: matches of ideas, not a one-to-one swap of APIs.

**Say:**
- This was the most important porting decision.
- Instead of bringing lwIP into NuttX, I matched each lwIP idea to a NuttX building block. lwIP's network interface becomes a NuttX net_driver_s. lwIP's packet buffer, pbuf, corresponds to NuttX's I/O buffer, iob. Adding an interface becomes registering a device, and I register wg0 as a tunnel-type device. Where lwIP calls an output callback to send a packet, NuttX calls the driver's txavail function, and the driver polls the stack with devif_poll. The wire under wg0 is a plain UDP socket.
- These are matches of ideas, not a one-to-one swap of APIs. The point is simple: use NuttX's own parts. Next, Version 1, the first working tunnel.

## 20 — Where It Was Hard: Six Bugs (14:37 → 15:46)

**On slide:** One table of all six bugs (bug / showed up in / lesson): 1 the receive thread stopped with the wg command / 2 ping works, TCP dies (in detail) / 3 "no real impact" was 10× (in detail) / 4 stack 94% full, saving the config froze the board, real boards only / 5 NuttX's own ChaCha20-Poly1305 (part 5) / 6 a 6 KB array on a 3 KB kernel stack (part 5). ## 21 — Bug ②: Ping Works. TCP Dies. (15:46 → 16:49) *(my favorite)*

**On slide:** Orange-red title. Left box SYMPTOM: ping 0% loss; the TCP handshake completes, then the connection dies; EBADF, "Bad file descriptor". Right box ROOT CAUSE & FIX: sends from wg_rx worked, sends from the system work queue failed; a file descriptor belongs to one task group; use psock_*() on a struct socket.

**Say:**
- This is my favorite bug in the project.
- On the same tunnel, ping was perfect, and even a TCP handshake finished, and then the connection died. When I added logging to the send path, it showed EBADF: bad file descriptor. All the sends that worked happened inside the receive task itself: handshake replies, keepalives, ping replies, the SYN-ACK, which is the second step of a TCP connection. The sends that failed came from the system work queue, which sends the TCP application data later.
- The reason: in NuttX, a file descriptor belongs to the task group that opened it. The file descriptor was not broken. It was just not visible from another task group. The fix was to stop using file descriptors and use NuttX's internal socket API. It works on a struct socket, which is just memory, so any task can use it. That is exactly why the kernel version keeps the socket as a struct socket.

## 22 — Bug ③: "No Real Impact" Was 10× Slower (16:49 → 17:31) *(cut candidate 3)*

**On slide:** Orange-red title. Two boxes: QUICK FIX, check every 50 ms (measured: about 10× slower) / REAL FIX, event-driven wake-up (psock_poll() callback and a semaphore).

**Say:**
- The third: something I never measured.
- The fix without file descriptors also broke poll, because poll works with file descriptors. So, as a quick fix, I checked the socket every fifty milliseconds. I thought the only cost was waking up the CPU when it was idle. I was wrong. Every packet that arrived waited up to fifty milliseconds, and that alone limited the speed: about ten times slower than it should have been. I thought it had no real cost, and it cost ten times.
- The real fix was a proper wait. The socket calls back and a semaphore wakes the thread up when data arrives.

## 25 — Version 2: WireGuard as a NuttX Kernel Driver (18:07 → 18:54)

**On slide:** Two panels: Version 1, an app (in apps/, uses kernel internals, FLAT only, never an in-tree driver) / Version 2, a kernel driver (drivers/net/wireguard, set up by a small wg command through ioctl, made for FLAT, PROTECTED and KERNEL). One line on the three build modes.

**Say:**
- Why did I move it into the kernel?
- Version one is an app. It lives in apps and reaches into socket internals of the kernel. That works in a FLAT build, where everything shares one memory space. But it can never be a proper in-tree driver, and it cannot work in PROTECTED or KERNEL builds, where apps and the kernel are kept apart.
- So I moved the device into the kernel, with the other network drivers, and a small wg command sets it up through ioctl (input/output control). Version one showed that the protocol works on NuttX. Version two is about fitting NuttX itself: a normal device, in every build mode, in a shape the community can review.

## 26 — The Kernel Design: The Big Picture (18:54 → 19:40)

**On slide:** A picture: user space holds only the wg command; an ioctl (one flat struct, no pointers; the private key is write-only) crosses the user/kernel boundary to wg0 in the kernel, which owns its UDP socket and RX thread, keeps timers / peers / cookies and uses NuttX crypto, between the NuttX network stack and the encrypted UDP to the peer.

**Say:**
- This is the shape of the real driver.
- In user space, there is only a small wg command. It sends settings to the kernel through ioctl. Each ioctl carries one flat struct with no pointers inside, so in PROTECTED and KERNEL builds the kernel never has to follow a pointer into user memory. The private key is write-only: no ioctl ever gives it back.
- In the kernel, wg0 is a normal network device. It owns its UDP socket and its receive thread, so the thread and socket bugs from version one cannot happen, by design. The crypto is NuttX's own. The protocol core is still derived from wireguard-lwip under BSD-3-Clause, and the LICENSE file says so.

## 27 — Bugs 5 & 6/6: Two More Bugs, One in NuttX Itself (19:40 → 20:47) *(locked with the kernel design)*

**On slide:** Two panels: NuttX's own chacha20poly1305 nonce bug (handshake works, data after the first packet fails; two-line crypto: fix) / a BUILD_KERNEL-only crash (6 KB array on a 3 KB kernel stack, moved to the heap). One line: SPRESENSE did not boot with CONFIG_RTC_HIRES on 13.0.1 / master; fixed locally, report drafted, not sent.

**Say:**
- On the way into the kernel, two more bugs came, and one of them was in NuttX itself.
- First, NuttX's own ChaCha20-Poly1305 wrote the counter into the wrong bytes of the nonce. When the counter is zero, both layouts are the same, so the handshake works, and then data packets after the first one fail to decrypt. Nothing in NuttX had called this code before, so nobody had hit it. It is a two-line fix, kept as its own crypto change.
- Second, a crash only in a kernel build. Setting the peers put a six-kilobyte array on a three-kilobyte kernel stack. In the simulator, with big stacks, it never showed up. It is the same stack lesson as bug four, one level deeper. I moved the array to the heap.
- One more from the board side: on NuttX 13.0.1 and master, SPRESENSE did not boot with the high-resolution RTC (real-time clock) option. I found the cause and fixed it locally. The report is drafted, but not sent.

**Say:**
- Let me be exact about which version ran where. There are three build modes, and I keep them apart.
- FLAT, on real boards. On October twelfth, I ran the exact series I prepared for upstream on the ESP32-S3 inside the StackChan: a handshake with Linux WireGuard, and ping both ways, up to the full packet size. ## 29 — Testing: What Ping Misses (22:30 → 23:28) *(core — do not cut)*

**On slide:** Three cards: same key on every boot? (SPRESENSE, 3 resets, 3 keys) / keys left after down? (sim, all zero) / a leak after hours? (sim, 151 rekeys, 100 down/up, nothing lost).

**Say:**
- This slide is about what a working tunnel cannot tell you: three problems that ping would never notice.
- First, keys. NuttX can seed its random device from build-time settings. Then a board makes the same private key every time it starts, and everything still works. So I reset a SPRESENSE three times and got three different keys. That is a quick check, not a proof of good randomness.
- Second, I read the device's memory in the simulator. After down, the session keys were all zero. The static key stays on purpose, so that up works again.
- Third, a long test, also in the simulator: a hundred and fifty-one forced rekeys and a hundred down-and-up cycles. No buffer was lost, and memory went back to exactly the start-up number. I am not saying it ran for a week, or that the simulator acts like the board.

## 30 — A Design Choice: The Timestamp Problem Has No Easy Answer (23:28 → 24:51) *(core — do not cut)*

**On slide:** The rule (a newer timestamp, even after a reboot), Chosen (real-time clock + the highest value this boot, plus a warning) / Not chosen (save every time, reserve a range in flash, wait), and the measured pair: clock unset → no handshake in 75 s, clock set → 4.1 s.

**Say:**
- This is the one place where I had to decide, not test. And then I measured my decision.
- WireGuard's replay protection has a rule: every handshake needs a newer timestamp than the peer has seen, even after a reboot. The device is usually the side that starts the handshake, and it is also the side least likely to have a clock with a battery.
- Saving every timestamp wears out the flash. Reserving a range in flash is the right long-term answer, but it needs a write guarantee I could not verify on these file systems. And just waiting means a device that ran for a month cannot reconnect for a month. Here, the easy choice and the right choice were not the same.
- So I chose the real-time clock plus the highest value used in this boot, exactly what Linux and wireguard-go do. And the driver warns once if the clock was never set.
- Then I measured it on the board. I sent the board nothing at all, because a ping from the peer would start the handshake for it. With the clock as it boots: no handshake in a seventy-five-second watch. With the clock set: four point one seconds. That is why the driver prints a warning.
